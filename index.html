Tirzepatide vs Retatrutide: what does 890% GIP potency actually mean?

You've seen the comparison — 100% vs 890% — and the tempting conclusion: "Retatrutide hits the GIP receptor 8.9× harder." That number is real pharmacology. The conclusion is where things go sideways. This page teaches you what potency actually measures, so the number finally makes sense.

⚖️

890% is a pharmacology number — not a clinical prediction

By the end of this page you'll know exactly what that 890% was measured from, why it can't be multiplied into weight loss, and why "low-dose Reta is just luxury Tirzepatide" misses the actual science.

01

The 100% vs 890% number

Where the famous comparison comes from — and what it is, and isn't.

People often see a comparison like this:

Tirzepatide — GIPR potency ≈ 100% of native GIP
Retatrutide — GIPR potency ≈ 890% of native GIP

…and naturally assume "Retatrutide hits the GIP receptor 8.9× harder" or "low-dose Reta is just a luxury Tirzepatide." That's a useful starting point — but it's also where things become misleading.

These numbers are a normalized pharmacology comparison, not a direct prediction of clinical results. Published structural and pharmacological work describes retatrutide as approximately 8.9-fold more potent at the human GIP receptor than native GIP in the relevant signaling comparison, while other reviews describe tirzepatide as having approximately native-GIP-like potency at GIPR. Cell/receptor assay

First major lesson: potency tells you how much drug is needed to produce a given level of receptor activation. It does not tell you how much weight someone will lose.

The two cars

Potency is about efficiency — how little input produces a given output. It says nothing about the final result once there's already plenty of input.

Before any dose comparisons, lock in four separate concepts. People constantly blend them:

POTENCY
"How much drug is needed to get a particular biological response?"
EFFICACY
"How much biological response can the drug ultimately produce?"
DOSE
"How much drug was actually given?"
CLINICAL EFFECT
"What actually happened to the person?"
Impossible-to-miss distinction: "890% potency" is not a universal property that can be directly converted into a human dose ratio. It is a normalized relative-potency figure from a particular experimental comparison. Likewise, "100%" is a reference normalization — not a statement that tirzepatide produces only 100% biological activity. Conceptual explanation
02

What does "100%" actually mean?

The reference point that makes the whole scale work.

Do not imagine that tirzepatide was officially assigned a universal "100% potency score." Instead: 100% is simply a convenient normalization point.

If native GIP — the body's own hormone — is represented as 100, and retatrutide produces the same signaling response at roughly one-ninth the concentration in the relevant assay, then retatrutide can be represented as approximately 890 on that normalized potency scale. Cell/receptor assay

100 → 200 → 400 → 890

"Higher potency means less concentration may be required to produce the same measured response."

Now the key mathematical concept: EC50. In plain language, EC50 is roughly the concentration required to produce 50% of the measured maximum response in that particular experimental system. The critical relationship: lower EC50 = higher potency. Retatrutide's GIPR EC50 sits far to the left of native GIP's — that's the entire 890% story, expressed as a curve position. ✕ 890% receptor occupancy   ✕ 890% biological effect   ✕ 890% weight loss
✕ 890% insulin release   ✕ 890% fat loss
It is a relative potency measurement from a specific assay — nothing more, nothing less.

03

Potency is not the same thing as dose

The confusion that causes most of the misunderstanding.

Someone might ask: "If retatrutide is 8.9× more potent at GIPR, shouldn't a tiny dose of retatrutide equal a much larger dose of tirzepatide?" That conclusion is too simplistic, because potency is only one link in a long chain:

  • Dose — the amount administered.
  • Potency — how much concentration may be needed to generate a particular response.
  • Pharmacokinetics — how much drug is present over time.
  • Receptor affinity & signaling properties — how the molecule engages the receptor.
  • Efficacy — the maximum response achievable in a particular system.

And the actual clinical outcome depends on the combined pharmacology of the whole drug.

The major lesson: you cannot look at potency alone and calculate the clinical effect.

04

So why does dose still matter?

Receptor activation, diminishing returns, and saturation.

Imagine a room containing 100 light switches (receptors). At very low drug concentration, only a few switches flip on. As concentration rises, more engage. Eventually the system approaches a point where adding more drug produces progressively smaller additional effects — the concentration-response curve flattens. This is saturation / diminishing returns.

The key idea: a more potent molecule can reach a particular receptor response at a lower concentration. But once enough drug is present to strongly engage that receptor, adding more doesn't necessarily produce proportionally more signaling.

Simplified conceptual model — receptor occupancy and downstream effect are related but not perfectly identical.

05

Now put tirzepatide into the picture

Why "100%" does not mean weak.

Tirzepatide is already a highly potent GIPR agonist. Published human-cell studies found its GIPR activity broadly comparable to native GIP in relevant assays, while its GLP-1R activity is weaker than native GLP-1. One study reported an EC50 of approximately 22.4 pM for tirzepatide versus 33.4 pM for GIP at GIPR in the cited signaling system — essentially the same neighborhood. Cell/receptor assay (EC50 values are assay-dependent: receptor expression, assay design, and signaling endpoint all shift the numbers.)

Teaching point: tirzepatide being "100%" does not mean tirzepatide is weak at GIPR. It means the reference scale makes its GIPR potency roughly comparable to the reference ligand — and the reference ligand is the body's own hormone, which is not weak.

Tirzepatide is an imbalanced dual agonist: it activates both GIPR and GLP-1R, with stronger relative activity at GIPR than at GLP-1R. Structural work found its GIPR binding ability equivalent to native GIP, with reduced potency at GLP-1R versus native GLP-1. Human pharmacology

Native GIP → 100  ·  Tirzepatide → roughly GIP-like  ·  Retatrutide → ~890

These numbers come from specific experimental comparisons — not universal clinical potency scores.

06

Retatrutide changes the receptor equation

Why it can't be described as "stronger tirzepatide."

Retatrutide is a triple agonist: GIPR + GLP-1R + GCGR. Its pharmacology is deliberately distributed across three receptors. The GIPR component is the eye-catching one — published structural work describes retatrutide as approximately 8.9× more potent than native GIP at GIPR, while its potency relative to the endogenous ligands at GLP-1R and GCGR is lower in the cited comparison: about 0.4× at GLP-1R and 0.3× at GCGR versus the native hormones. Cell/receptor assay

The critical concept: retatrutide isn't simply "tirzepatide with more GIP." It changes the overall receptor profile — different receptor profile → different integrated pharmacology.

07

The 890% number does not mean 8.9× the clinical effect

The most important chapter on this page.

"If retatrutide has 890% GIP potency versus 100% for tirzepatide, does that mean 8.9× more GIP effect?"

No.

Here's why, point by point:

  • It's a relative potency vs native GIP in a particular experimental system — not a head-to-head clinical effect measurement between retatrutide and tirzepatide.
  • Tirzepatide is already highly active at GIPR — near-native potency means the receptor is already being driven hard.
  • What reaches the receptor depends on pharmacokinetics and dose — not just the molecule's intrinsic potency.
  • Signaling isn't linear — more potency ≠ proportionally more effect (Chapter 4's saturation).
  • Retatrutide's effects come from three pathways, not GIPR alone — GIPR potency can't explain the whole drug.
  • Physiology has ceilings and feedback — downstream systems push back.
POTENCY+DOSE+EXPOSURE+RECEPTOR PROFILE+SIGNALING+PHYSIOLOGY=CLINICAL EFFECT

08

How dosing covers a wide range of receptor signaling

One molecule, multiple response curves.

When a dose is increased, the body doesn't get a separate "GIP dose," "GLP-1 dose," and "glucagon dose." You administer the molecule. It distributes through the body. Its concentration changes over time. Different receptors respond according to their own pharmacological characteristics.

Why this matters for multi-receptor drugs: dose escalation can move the drug farther along multiple receptor-response curves simultaneously — changing the overall pharmacological profile, not just making one receptor "stronger."

Why 1 mg, 4 mg, 8 mg and 12 mg are not just "more of the same"

Dose levels move you along curves — they don't rescale potency.

Different clinical doses of retatrutide have been studied, and higher doses generally produce greater drug exposure and greater average metabolic effects in clinical trials. Human clinical data

But: a higher dose is not simply turning the GIP receptor from 890% to 1,780%. That is not how potency works. Instead:

Potency = the position of the response curve.
Dose / exposure = where you are operating on that curve.

Once a pathway is strongly engaged, increasing the dose can produce smaller incremental changes in that pathway — while still increasing exposure across the other pathways. That's why dose escalation changes the balance of a multi-receptor drug, not just its volume.

The GIP receptor itself: what is it actually doing?

Zooming into the biology behind the number.

GIP = glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide — an endogenous incretin hormone. When the GIP receptor is activated, signaling can influence pancreatic beta cells and other tissues:

GIP / GIP-like agonist → GIP receptor → Gs/cAMP signaling → cellular signaling changes → physiological effects

GIP biology is more complicated than "GIP = insulin." GIP receptor signaling acts in multiple tissues, and the overall metabolic outcome depends on the physiological context — and on which other receptors are being activated at the same time.

12

Potency, efficacy, affinity, occupancy, dose — the five terms people mix up

A standalone reference. Tap each term.

Scientific definitionPotency describes how much of a drug is needed to produce a defined level of response, and it is typically quantified as the EC50 — the concentration that produces half of the maximal measured response in a particular experimental system. A lower EC50 means higher potency, because less drug concentration is required to reach the same response. Potency is always relative to the assay it was measured in: receptor expression levels, the signaling endpoint chosen, and experimental conditions can all shift the number. It describes the position of the concentration-response curve, not its height.
Plain EnglishPotency is a measure of efficiency — it tells you how little of a drug is needed to get a certain amount of effect. Scientists usually express it as the EC50, which is simply the concentration that produces half of the maximum response seen in that particular lab test. A more potent drug needs a lower concentration to reach the same response, which is why its curve sits further to the left on a graph. But the number only applies to the exact test setup it came from, since different lab conditions can change it. And potency says nothing about how large the effect can ultimately become — only how little drug it takes to get partway there.
Scientific definitionEfficacy, in pharmacology, refers to the maximum response a drug can produce in a given system once the receptor is fully engaged — the ceiling of the concentration-response curve, often called Emax. It is distinct from potency: a drug can be highly potent, meaning active at tiny concentrations, yet have modest efficacy, meaning a low ceiling — or the reverse. Efficacy depends on how well the drug-receptor complex drives downstream signaling, not merely on how tightly the drug binds. Comparing two drugs fairly requires looking at both numbers together, never one alone.
Plain EnglishEfficacy is about the ceiling — the biggest response a drug can possibly produce in a given test system, no matter how much you add. It is completely separate from potency: a drug can start working at a tiny dose but still top out at a modest effect, while another might need a larger dose yet ultimately reach a higher ceiling. What sets the ceiling is how well the drug-receptor pairing can actually drive the cell's signaling machinery, not merely how tightly the drug holds on. So to compare two drugs fairly, you need to look at both their potency and their efficacy side by side.
Scientific definitionAffinity describes how strongly a molecule binds to its receptor — the tendency of the drug and receptor to associate, usually expressed as a dissociation constant (Kd) or inhibition constant (Ki). Higher affinity means the molecule is found occupying the receptor at lower concentrations. Crucially, binding tightly does not guarantee strong activation: a molecule can have high affinity yet act as a weak partial agonist, or even as an antagonist that blocks the receptor. Affinity governs occupancy; a separate property, efficacy, governs what happens after occupancy.
Plain EnglishAffinity is grip strength — how tightly a drug molecule holds onto its receptor, usually reported as a Kd or Ki value. The tighter the grip, the lower the concentration needed for the drug to be found sitting on the receptor. But holding on tightly is not the same as switching the receptor on strongly: a molecule can cling to a receptor yet only weakly activate it, or even block it from working at all. In short, affinity decides how much of the receptor gets occupied, while a separate property — efficacy — decides what happens once it is.
Scientific definitionReceptor occupancy is the fraction of the available receptor population that is bound by ligand at a given moment. It is determined by the drug's concentration relative to its affinity: as concentration rises, occupancy climbs toward saturation in a hyperbolic relationship. Occupancy is a prerequisite for signaling but is not the same as signaling — downstream amplification, spare receptors, and desensitization mean the response curve need not mirror the occupancy curve. Two drugs can therefore produce equal occupancy while causing very different functional effects.
Plain EnglishOccupancy is simply the share of receptors that currently have a drug molecule sitting on them. It goes up as the drug concentration rises relative to how tightly the drug binds, leveling off as the receptors fill up. But occupied does not automatically mean activated in equal measure: the cell can amplify the signal, dampen it, or grow tired of it, so the response doesn't have to track occupancy one-to-one. That is why two drugs can fill the same fraction of receptors yet produce quite different effects.
Scientific definitionDose is the amount of drug administered — for example, milligrams injected per week. Dose is only the starting input: pharmacokinetics, meaning absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion, determine what concentration actually reaches the receptors over time, which scientists call exposure. The same nominal dose can therefore produce different exposures in different people, or with different formulations. Clinical effects track with exposure at the target tissue, not with the dose number alone.
Plain EnglishDose is what you put in — the amount of drug actually given, such as milligrams per weekly injection. But the dose is only the beginning of the story: the body absorbs, distributes, breaks down, and clears the drug over time, and all of that determines how much drug the receptors truly see, which scientists call exposure. Because of these steps, the same labeled dose can lead to different exposures in different people or with different formulations. What ultimately matters for the effect is the exposure at the target — not the number on the label by itself.
Scientific definitionClinical effect is the outcome actually observed in humans — changes in body weight, blood sugar, blood pressure, or adverse events as measured in clinical trials. It is the end product of the entire chain: the drug's potency and efficacy at each receptor, the dose given, the resulting exposure over time, the full receptor profile engaged, intracellular signaling, and the body's physiological feedback systems. No single pharmacological parameter, including an impressive receptor potency figure, can predict it on its own. Only controlled human trials can establish what a drug actually does in people.
Plain EnglishClinical effect is what really happened to real people — the weight change, blood sugar change, or side effects measured in proper clinical trials. It is the final result of everything chained together: how potent the drug is at each receptor, how much was given, how much reached the receptors over time, which receptors were switched on, how the cells signaled, and how the body pushed back. No single lab number — not even a striking potency figure — can predict this on its own. The only way to know what a drug truly does in humans is to test it in humans.

Two sentences worth memorizing:

"A molecule can have high affinity without producing the same downstream response as another molecule."

"Potency, affinity and efficacy are related concepts, but they are not interchangeable."

13

Myths, reality, and the big takeaway

Six myths, corrected — then the final visual.

MYTH: "890% potency means retatrutide is 8.9× stronger than tirzepatide clinically."
REALITY:
The 8.9× figure is relative GIPR potency versus native GIP in a specific experimental comparison. It does not predict an 8.9× clinical effect. Cell/receptor assay
MYTH: "100% means tirzepatide barely activates GIPR."
REALITY:
Tirzepatide is already a potent GIPR agonist, described as having native-GIP-like potency at GIPR in relevant human-cell experiments. 100% is the reference point, not a grade. Human pharmacology
MYTH: "Higher potency means you automatically need a lower clinical dose."
REALITY:
Clinical dose depends on far more than intrinsic receptor potency: pharmacokinetics, exposure, duration, formulation, therapeutic window, tolerability, and the desired clinical effect. Conceptual explanation
MYTH: "If retatrutide is 8.9× more potent at GIPR, it must activate GIPR 8.9× more at every dose."
REALITY:
Potency changes the concentration-response relationship. It does not create a permanent 8.9× multiplier at every concentration — saturation compresses differences at high exposure. Conceptual explanation
MYTH: "Retatrutide's GIP receptor is the reason it works better."
REALITY:
Retatrutide is a triple agonist. Its overall effects arise from integrated GIPR, GLP-1R, and GCGR pharmacology — not one receptor. Mechanistic interpretation
MYTH: "More receptor activation always means better results."
REALITY:
Biology has dose-response curves, ceilings, feedback mechanisms, tolerability limits, and trade-offs. More signal is not always more benefit. Mechanistic interpretation
